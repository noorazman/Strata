The history of inference engines on consumer hardware is a story of constraints giving rise to creativity. A model with hundreds of billions of parameters cannot fit on a single graphics card, so the engine must decide, token by token, which part of the network lives where: on the fast memory of the accelerator, in the slower but larger memory of the host, or on the storage that is large but patient. Every design decision in such a system is a trade between the latency of a transfer and the cost of the arithmetic it avoids.

Mixture-of-experts architectures changed the shape of that trade. Instead of one giant feed-forward network that every token must pass through, the model routes each token to a small handful of specialists. The routing is cheap: a small gate network reads the hidden state and selects a dozen experts from a field of thousands. The experts themselves are the bulk of the parameters, and they are the part of the model that varies most from token to token. This locality of use is the whole reason the hybrid memory scheme works at all: the experts that one token needs are almost never the same experts that the next token needs, and the ones that do repeat are the ones the cache should keep.

The cache policy is therefore the center of the design. A small cache held on the accelerator avoids the transfer for the experts that repeat, which in practice is a minority of the lookups. A large cache costs memory that the rest of the model needs, and the memory on the accelerator is the scarcest resource in the whole system. The profile-driven policy sidesteps the question of what will be needed: it ranks the expert positions by how often they were selected in a recorded workload, and fills the cache with that ranking. The ranking is cheap to compute and cheap to store, and it turns a per-token decision into a one-time decision at startup.

There is a subtlety in measuring such a system, because the numbers that the hardware reports are not the numbers that matter. The accelerator utilization reported by the driver is a fraction of the wall clock during which at least one kernel was running, and it says nothing about how much of the chip is busy, or whether the kernel that is running is the kernel that should be running. The same is true of host utilization: a thread that is spinning on a lock counts as busy, and a thread that is waiting for a transfer counts as busy in the same number. The honest measurement is the time of the work, measured at the level of the work: the milliseconds that the pool spent computing expert outputs, the milliseconds that the accelerator spent running its graphs, the milliseconds that the transfers took, and the gaps between them, where nothing is doing anything useful.

Transfers are the silent cost. The path from host memory to accelerator memory is fast per byte but slow per access, and the system is made of many small accesses rather than few large ones. Each expert that is not resident must be fetched, and the fetch is issued from a host thread that is also the thread driving the rest of the step. The transfer overlaps with the computation of the other experts, which is the entire point of the parallel design: the pool computes what is already on the accelerator while the misses are on their way. The quality of the overlap is the quality of the system, and it degrades gracefully as the miss rate rises, because the miss work is independent of the hit work by construction.

The accelerator side of the model is itself a study in layers. The attention layers are split into two kinds: the recurring kind, which maintains a running state and needs only a little memory per step, and the scanning kind, which looks back over the whole sequence with a sparse selection of the positions that matter. The scanning kind is the expensive one, because the cost of the selection grows with the length of the sequence, and the sequences in the production configuration are long. The engine measures each stage of each layer separately, because the total is not a useful number: the total says that the layer took a given time, and the stage table says which part of the layer took it, and those are different questions with different answers.

Speculation is the second axis of the design, and it is the axis that hides the first. The model carries inside itself a small draft network that guesses the next few tokens, and the main network verifies the guesses in a single pass. When the guess is right, the cost of the token is the cost of the verification, which is cheaper than the cost of the full generation, and the verification of several tokens is cheaper per token than the generation of one. When the guess is wrong, the round is shorter, and the system falls back to the ordinary path. The acceptance rate of the draft is therefore the efficiency of the speculation, and the efficiency of the speculation is the distance between the measured speed and the speed that the arithmetic alone would give.

Measuring the arithmetic alone is the first step of the investigation, because it sets the floor. The floor is the time that the accelerator needs to run its work for one token, with no host involvement at all: the graphs replay back to back, the host is out of the loop, and the only cost is the cost of the silicon. Whatever the wall clock exceeds that floor by is the cost of the host, and the host cost is where the optimization lives, because the silicon is what it is and the host is what it can be made. The gap between the two numbers is the size of the prize, and the structure of the gap, broken down into its phases, is the map of the work.

The map has known features. The routing is on the accelerator and is cheap. The selection of the experts is on the host and is cheap. The computation of the expert outputs is on the host and is the bulk of the host work: the dequantization of the compressed weights, the multiplication of the activation by the dequantized weights, and the reduction of the result. The dequantization is the interesting part of that work, because the weights are stored in a compressed form that trades a little arithmetic for a lot of memory, and the arithmetic of the decompression is what the host spends its time on. The arithmetic is vectorized to the width of the machine, and the width of this machine is the width of the previous generation, which is half the width of the current one, and the cost of the decompression is proportional to the width that is missing.

All of this is to say that the optimization must be measured before it is attempted, because the system is made of many small costs and the small costs hide the large one. The investigation proceeds from the outside in: first the wall clock and the utilization of the machine as a whole, then the time of the pool and the time of the accelerator, then the time of the individual phases, and finally the time of the individual kernels. At each level the question is the same: where is the time going, and is the time that is going there the time that should be going there. The answer at one level becomes the question at the next, and the work is done when the answer at the last level is a number that matches the arithmetic, or a number that has been paid for with a reason.

The reason must be written down, because the next person who touches this system will not have the context, and the context is the difference between a change that is an improvement and a change that is a guess. The documentation of a measurement is not a log of the measurement: it is the log, plus the conditions under which it was taken, plus the interpretation, plus the decision that followed. The log is reproducible, and the interpretation is what makes it useful, and the decision is what makes it true. A number without its conditions is a rumour, and a rumour without its decision is decoration, and the purpose of the investigation is to end the decoration and leave the fact.
